## [Terence Tao says AI has changed mathematics and the field must adapt](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/01/mathematics-has-changed-lets-act-like-it/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

In a blog post titled "Mathematics has changed. Let's act like it," Fields Medalist Terence Tao argues that AI tools have fundamentally altered how mathematical research is actually practiced, and urges the community to revise its norms accordingly. He focuses specifically on how credit is assigned, how proofs get digested and verified, and how research directions are chosen. Tao is one of the most influential mathematicians alive, so his claim that AI has pushed mathematics from a craft toward an industrial discipline carries real weight in ongoing debates over authorship, peer review, hiring, and career incentives. If the field adopts his proposed norms, what counts as a genuine mathematical contribution — and who gets rewarded for it — could shift substantially. Central to Tao's argument is "digestion": an AI-generated proof only becomes useful once someone understands it deeply enough to build on it, so he suggests substantial credit should go to whoever digests, verifies, and extends a result even if they supplied no new step of the original proof. He also notes that machine-written proofs can be misleadingly uniform in tone, presenting routine and genuinely difficult steps as equally easy to follow.

**Background**: Automated theorem proving is a long-established subfield of automated reasoning in which computer programs search for formal proofs of mathematical statements; historically it has worked best on narrow, heavily formalized problems. Recently, LLM-based systems have begun producing proofs of open research problems, which is the immediate spur for this essay. Tao has addressed the topic at length elsewhere, including his paper and ICM lecture "Mathematics in the age of AI," where he argues that AI's success rests on the canonical theories human mathematicians painstakingly built over centuries.

**Discussion**: Hacker News commenters were broadly sympathetic: one summarized the emerging consensus that results themselves are now cheap while digestion is the scarce contribution, and another framed the shift as mathematics passing from an age of craftsmanship to an age of industrial production, arguing people still are not dreaming big enough about the tool's potential. Others pointed to a large body of mathematician commentary complaining that AI proofs are ugly, long, and incomprehensible — countered by the observation that first proofs of major results have always been like that, with elegance arriving later — and questioned why so many discussions assume AI capabilities will stop at theorem proving rather than extending to choosing problems and explaining results.
